### 1.1 Constructivism
- **Constructivism** (Piaget): knowledge is a rebuilt mental model, not a download. You can recite the right physics fact and still feel that heavy objects fall faster.
- **Assimilation** (Piaget): fit new experience into the old mental model. Call the contradictory demo a trick so you never change your mind.
- **Accommodation** (Piaget): change the mental model to fit new experience. After you drop a heavy ball and a light ball and they hit together, the old model has to change.
- **Disequilibrium** (Piaget): the mismatch between your model and reality that forces learning. Your drawing comes out wrong and that wrong result itself makes you rethink the rule.
- **More knowledgeable other (MKO)** (Vygotsky): a person or tool that can do what the learner cannot yet. A tutor, a stronger peer, or software that gives the next hint.
- **Zone of proximal development (ZPD)** (Vygotsky): what you can do with help but not alone yet. Scaffolding is temporary help that should disappear as you get better, like a full worksheet, then a reminder, then nothing.
- **Kirschner, Sweller, and Clark (2006):** learning being constructive does not mean teaching should be unguided. Beginners need strong guidance; freer exploration helps more after they already know a lot.

### 1.2 Constructionism
- **Constructionism** (Papert): learn by building something real that others can see or use. Making a game or a shared world teaches by making your thinking visible.
- **Constructivism vs constructionism:** constructivism rebuilds a model in your head; constructionism drives learning by making something outside your head. Inside rebuild versus public make.
- **Instructionism** (Papert): improve learning mainly by improving how teachers explain and lecture. Better telling is treated as the whole fix.
- **Wide walls** (Resnick): support many kinds of personally meaningful projects, not just many ages or devices. Same tool lets one student make a story and another make a game.
- **Low floor / high ceiling** (Resnick / Papert): easy for a beginner to start, and still deep enough for an expert to keep going. A first drag-and-drop project and a complex later project on the same tool.
- **Four P's** (Resnick): Projects, Passion, Peers, Play. Learn by making things you care about, with others, in a playful way.
- **Body-syntonic** (Papert): understand the program by acting it out with your own body. Walk the path yourself, then write the steps that match what you walked.

### 1.3 Direct instruction
- **Lecture** (etymology): the word comes from Latin for reading. Historically a teacher reading material aloud to students.
- **Direct instruction vs discovery:** false dichotomy; the real variable is how expert the learner already is. Guide beginners closely; loosen guidance as skill grows.
- **Faultless communication** (Engelmann): if the student has not learned, the instruction has not taught. Treat confusion as a flaw in the teaching materials, not the student.
- **Lowercase di vs capital-D Direct Instruction:** di means teacher-led teaching in general; DI means one specific scripted program by Engelmann and Becker. Everyday teacher-led versus that named program.
- **Gradual release** (Pearson and Gallagher): I do, then we do, then you do. Teacher shows one fully worked problem, then solves with the class, then students solve alone.

### 1.4 Behaviorism
- **Operant conditioning** (Skinner): shape voluntary behavior by what happens after it. Reward closer and closer versions of the target action until the full action appears.
- **Classical conditioning** (Pavlov): pair two stimuli until an automatic response transfers. Ring a bell with food until the bell alone makes the dog drool; tests can pair with stress the same way.
- **Positive reinforcement:** add something liked so the behavior goes up. Give praise or food after the desired action.
- **Negative reinforcement:** remove something disliked so the behavior goes up. Turn off a phone time-limit after chores are done. This increases the chore behavior; it is not punishment.
- **Positive punishment:** add something disliked so the behavior goes down. Add extra chores after the unwanted action.
- **Negative punishment:** remove something liked so the behavior goes down. Take away dessert when dinner is unfinished.
- **Reinforcement vs punishment:** reinforcement tries to increase a behavior; punishment tries to decrease it. Positive means add something; negative means take something away. Ask up-or-down first, then add-or-remove.
- **Why cognitivism displaced behaviorism:** behaviorism could not explain abstract ideas, transfer to new problems, or truly novel solving. Watching only outward actions left the mind unexplained.
- **Immediate reinforcement:** the consequence must land right after the behavior. Instant right/wrong after each tiny step beats feedback a week later.

### 1.5 Cognitivism
- **Ecological cognition** (Neisser): study memory and perception in messy real life, not only the lab. How people remember in everyday settings, not only clean experiments.
- **Newell and Simon:** people build an internal picture of the problem and search possible moves. Think through a maze of mental states, not only react to a stimulus.
- **Cognitivist feedback:** saying only “wrong, try again” mainly nudges the next click; saying why it was wrong updates the mental model. Fix the understanding, not just the answer.
- **Cognitive load** (Sweller): intrinsic is how hard the content is; extraneous is useless clutter; germane is useful thinking work. Remove decorative junk and sequence hard ideas.
- **Image Principle** (Mayer): adding the instructor’s face to a narrated lesson usually does not improve learning. The face rarely adds new information and can steal attention.
- **Coherence Principle** (Mayer): visuals that do not teach the idea can hurt learning. Cute unrelated animations waste limited attention.

### 1.6 Metacognition
- **Self-reflection** (Zimmerman): after the work, judge how it went and why. Plan before, do the work, then ask what caused the result. SRL = self-regulated learning.
- **Fluency illusion:** feeling smooth after rereading is not the same as being able to recall it. Notes feel familiar, then the quiz is blank; a practice quiz exposes that.
- **Monitoring needs capacity:** stress can use up the attention you need to notice you are lost. After the pressure drops, you suddenly see what you missed.

### 1.7 Sociocultural
- **Guided participation** (Rogoff): learn by watching and joining real activity beside more experienced people. Help cook the meal; do not only hear a talk about cooking.
- **Legitimate peripheral participation** (Lave and Wenger): a newcomer does real but low-stakes work at the edge of a group, then moves toward the center. First do simple real tasks the group actually needs, then harder ones.
- **Honor cultural variation:** allow more than one valid way to reason or represent an idea. Do not assume one style of thinking is the only correct one.
- **ZPD vs scaffolding:** ZPD is the gap between alone and with help; scaffolding is the temporary support inside that gap. Zone of proximal development = the zone; scaffolding = the help in the zone.

### 1.8 Motivation
- **Mastery-oriented** (goal orientation): success means actually understanding, not looking better than others. “I got it” beats “I looked smart.”
- **ESPN Top 10 / Not Top 10:** performance-approach tries to make the highlight reel; performance-avoidance tries to avoid the blooper reel. The same dunk attempt can become either.
- **Belonging** (Walton and Cohen): tell students that feeling out of place is normal and temporary. That message can raise motivation and grades.
- **Self-determination** (Deci and Ryan): motivation needs autonomy, competence, and relatedness. Meaningful choice, a sense I can do this, and connection to people.

### 1.9 Ethics
- **Remote proctoring** (lesson cite): the systems struggled more with darker-skinned faces. People forced to use the tool get uneven harm.
- **AI feedback and demographics:** girls and underrepresented minorities got less critical feedback. Softer comments are not equal help.
- **EdTech Matthew effect** (Reich): poorly designed tech helps students who are already ahead first. The gap grows before the tool helps everyone.
- **Learning styles** (visual / auditory / kinesthetic): matching teaching to a preferred “style” is a myth. What actually differs is prior knowledge, language, access, and disability.
- **Assessment:** measure this student’s learning to give a grade or feedback. Read one person’s heart rate.
- **Evaluation:** use those measures to judge whether a tool or curriculum works. Compare two workout plans using the same heart-rate readings.

## CS6795 — Lessons 1–3 + Thagard Ch. 1–4

No Honorlock. Quizzes are open notes. This file still has to *make sense* or the quiz is guesswork. Book: Thagard, *Mind*, 2nd ed., 2005.

### CRUM (Computational-Representational Understanding of Mind)

- **Plain:** Thinking is “structures that stand for things” plus “steps that run on those structures.” Like data plus algorithms, for the mind. Not only logic — neural-style nets count too.
- **Quiz voice:** Representational structures + computational procedures. Connectionism still counts. **Multifarious** (no one type is enough), **successful**, **incomplete** (needs brains + social — his Part II). Six types, in order: logic, rules, concepts, analogies, images, connections.

### Explanation schema (every chapter)

- **Plain:** To explain a smart behavior: people have representations of type X, processes Y on them, and Y applied to X produces the behavior.

### Five criteria (how to grade an approach)

- **Plain:** Does it have enough kinds of idea? Can it plan, decide, explain, learn, do language? Does it match psychology? Brains? Can you actually use it?
- **Quiz voice:** representational power; computational power; psychological plausibility; neurological plausibility; practical applicability.

### Mind–computer–brain

- **Plain:** A program is data structures + algorithms. A mind, in this course, is representations + procedures. The third comparison is the brain (especially connectionism). Rough analogy — not “the brain is a PC.” Serial vs parallel.

### Marr’s three levels (lecture, not Thagard Ch. 1)

- **Plain:** You can ask (1) what problem is being solved, (2) what recipe and data, (3) what hardware. Do not answer “what is the problem?” with “neurotransmitters.”
- **Quiz voice:** **Computational** = what problem. **Algorithmic** = algorithms + data structures. **Implementation** = neurons / silicon. Thagard’s own map is CRUM + six types + five criteria, not Marr.

### Logic (Ch. 2)

- **Plain:** Ideas as sentences you can prove from. “If A then B; A; so B.” You can also guess a hidden cause to *explain* a clue (Holmes), or generalize from examples.
- **Quiz voice:** Predicate-logic-like sentences; **deductive and inductive** procedures. **Modus ponens** (if A then B; A; therefore B). **Abduction** (Peirce): hypothesize in order to *explain* (Frank didn’t show → maybe sick). **Induction:** narrow = generalize from instances; broad = any uncertain inference (abduction counts). **Bayes** for P(hypothesis | evidence). Thagard: logic is a strong *tool*; he is not sure it is the *core* of human thought. Lecture: Holmes clip = abduction.

### Probability / Monty Hall (lecture)

- **Plain:** If we used the math we would switch doors (~2/3). Most people stay. So the mind may not “run probability,” even though AI does.
- **Quiz voice:** **Bounded rationality** (Simon): incomplete info + limited compute → **satisficing** (good enough, not perfect).

### Rules (Ch. 3 + lecture)

- **Plain:** Lots of “if this, then that,” plus hunting through possible moves, plus making *new* if-thens. Looks like logic’s if-then; it is not the same as proving modus ponens.
- **Quiz voice:** Mental rules + **search** + procedures that **form new rules**. Newell/Simon; Anderson **ACT**; **SOAR**. **Domain/world** knowledge vs **control/strategy** (control is often unspoken). **Heuristics** = rules of thumb (availability heuristic). **Power law of practice** (Thagard).

### Concepts (Ch. 4 + lecture)

- **Plain:** A concept is not a perfect dictionary definition. It is a bundle of typical features. This red light in front of you is a **percept**; **red-as-stop** is the concept.
- **Quiz voice:** Drop **classical** strict definitions. Typical features; **approximate match**. **Schemas/scripts** = bigger bundles. Slots, kind/part hierarchies; spreading activation, matching, **inheritance**. Quiz-bait types: **axiomatic** / **prototypical** / **exemplar** / **qualia**.
